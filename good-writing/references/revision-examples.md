# Revision examples

All situations and passages below are invented for this skill. They are not quotations from Graham or reports of actual findings. Each example states the material available to the editor; the revision must stay within it.

## 1. Repetition reveals one thought written twice

Available material: only the draft below. No research or outcome data is supplied.

**Draft**

> I take notes to record what I am thinking. Once I have written those thoughts down, I can return to what I thought at the time. The purpose of my notes, then, is to let my future self revisit my past thoughts.

**Revision**

> I take notes so I can revisit my thoughts later.

**What changed:** The supposed conclusion merely repeated the opening. Compression exposes that there is one idea here. If the author wants to argue that rereading changes their thinking, they need to develop that additional idea; the editor should not invent it to make the paragraph seem deeper.

## 2. Expansion makes an omitted relationship explicit

Available material: a fictional newsletter promises to publish on Tuesday and Thursday. The author's concern is that an unpredictable schedule makes it harder for subscribers to know when to look for an issue. No evidence about audience growth is supplied.

**Draft**

> A fixed publishing schedule matters because it makes this newsletter more consistent.

**Revision**

> We promise to publish every Tuesday and Thursday so readers know when to look for a new issue. The consistency we want is in their expectations.

**What changed:** Repeating stability did not explain the point. A slightly longer version makes the intended relationship visible using the supplied context. It does not claim that the schedule increases readership.

## 3. Preserve the complication that carries the idea

Available material: a fictional export tool offers a preview. The author argues that it is useful for hard-to-reverse exports but burdensome for temporary copies. No user study or measured time saving is supplied.

**Draft**

> A preview is useful for exports, which are sometimes hard to undo, although other exports are just temporary copies, so requiring a preview for exports can be useful but can also be unnecessary.

**Tempting compression**

> Always preview exports before proceeding.

**Revision**

> A preview earns its place when an export is hard to undo; when the export only creates a temporary copy, requiring that preview adds a step without addressing the same risk.

**What changed:** The revision organizes the conditions into one relationship. The longer sentence carries a distinction the compressed version destroys. It remains the author's design argument, not a measured finding.

## 4. Give a branching thought a readable path

Available material: the author prefers meetings for resolving disagreement, believes status updates can be sent beforehand, and wants meeting time reserved for discussion. These are proposals, not demonstrated productivity results.

**Notes**

> Meetings should resolve disagreements. Updates matter too. Send updates before the meeting. Some topics need discussion. A meeting should leave room for that discussion.

**Revision**

> Use the meeting to resolve disagreements that need discussion. Send status updates beforehand, so the meeting can begin with the questions still open.

**What changed:** The revision gives the update a supporting role in the argument instead of alternating between two topics. It does not add a claim that this practice has already shortened meetings.
